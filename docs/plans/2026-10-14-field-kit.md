## The one idea

When the internet is gone, Zimi tells you **where you are, what the sky is doing, and what to do next**.

Everything below is one of those three, and they answer each other: the Field Guide says "build shelter before dark", and the sky beside it says dark is in 2 h 14 min; Earth puts the guide's water sources on the map.

- **Where you are:** Zimi Earth (look down).
- **What the sky is doing:** the world-class sky (look up; the Almanac stays the Easter egg and is the engine under both).
- **What to do next:** the Field Guide, plus a best-answer card in search that runs on a Pi.

## 1. The sky (built first; everything else stands on it)

Today: a Canvas 2D panorama with real astronomy (within 1° of JPL Horizons) painted with hand-picked colours: one vertical gradient from a `SKY_TONES` table, 517 stars in five colour buckets, a Milky Way of three soft strokes, a Moon drawn 10x its size, a beach with whales. The astronomy is world class; the picture is not.

The window Eric saw (most likely AgentSystemLabs/agent-office) looks better because its sky lights a scene and moves every frame, with stylised physics and forecast weather. We go the other way: **every pixel is the real sky at your place, right now**, which no forecast-free toy can claim and which is exactly what a survivor needs.

In order:

1. **Stars:** all 9,096 BSC5 stars (already shipped for the 3D Earth, 54 KB, public domain), continuous colour from B-V, size and brightness from magnitude, twinkle from airmass. No new bytes.
2. **Milky Way:** the ESO all-sky panorama (CC BY 4.0, credited), ~150-300 KB, with dust lanes and both Magellanic Clouds.
3. **Light:** physically based scattering (Hillaire 2020: three small tables computed on the GPU at start, ~12 KB of shader, nothing downloaded). Twilight's blue hour, the Earth's shadow and the Belt of Venus at dusk, the sky brightening around a full Moon: all from the Sun's and Moon's real positions. Canvas 2D stays as the fallback for devices without WebGL2 and while the 3D Earth holds the GPU.
4. **Point at the sky:** on a phone, the view follows the compass and gyro. Hold it up, see what that star is, which way north is, where the Sun will rise. The feature that turns a picture into a tool. Earth and the sky are one object: tilt the phone down and you are looking at the globe where you stand, tilt it up and you are looking at the sky above it. Two apps would be two doors to the same place.
   - Motion permission is asked on the first tap (iOS requires it), never on load; a compass that needs calibrating says so in one line; without sensors, dragging does the same.
5. **Night detail:** airglow, zodiacal light, a darkness dial defaulting to dark (no lights after a grid failure), earthshine, the Moon at true size, lunar eclipse colour, all 88 constellations, the brightest nebulae and galaxies, planet phases.
6. **Night mode is red.** After dark the whole sky view, and the Field Guide with it, can turn deep red on black, as astronomers and soldiers use, so looking at the phone does not cost your night vision. It also spends the least battery on an OLED screen.
7. **Rendered on demand.** A still sky draws once and again only when something in it has moved a pixel; a phone that has to last the night is not spent on a frame loop.
8. **The beach, with its life, stays the default; the true horizon is a choice** (reversed 2026-10-14: a first look of stars alone was not what Eric wanted). The beach is lit by the physical sky, and each moving thing is judged against what is really seen.

Never: weather. Offline we cannot know it, and a guessed cloud is a lie.

Without a location the sky says where it thinks you are (from the time zone) and asks once; every fact that depends on it carries that place's name.

Survivor's use, shown as plain facts beside the sky: daylight left, twilight ends at, moonrise and how much light it gives, true north from the Sun or Polaris (or the Southern Cross), local solar time.

## 2. Zimi Earth (the app you open on purpose)

The Almanac's 3D Earth as a destination: drag the globe, zoom to a place, and the library meets you there.

- Wikipedia articles with coordinates appear as you zoom (Nearby).
- Wikivoyage's guide, the local language and phrasebook (Travel).
- The installed maps take over below a zoom level.
- Day and night, the terminator, the real sky above any point (tap a place, look up).
- Field Guide's hazards for the region (from Wikivoyage "Stay safe" and ready.gov).

Built on what exists: three.js and the Earth already ship; the Q-ID and coordinate data already resolve. To verify before building: which Wikipedia builds keep article coordinates.

## 3. The Field Guide (first aid and how to stay alive)

The bar (Eric, 2026-09-28): the app must beat the ZIM's own pages. The sources are PDFs and crawls, so the app wins by changing the way in, not by re-hosting text.

- **Reachable at the moment of need.** Nobody browses an apps row while someone bleeds. Search is where everyone already goes: typing "bleeding", "tourniquet", "water", "lost" or "hypothermia" puts that Field Guide card first, as the best answer. The home page carries one quiet "Help now" entry. The best answer card (section 4) and the Field Guide are one surface.
- **Start from the situation:** someone is bleeding; I need water; I need warmth; I'm lost; I need to signal; a disaster is coming.
- **Each opens one card,** short, in the order you act (Rule of Threes, STOP, PACE, and MARCH for trauma). The essential steps live in the card itself, a few KB inside Zimi, so the guide works with no ZIM installed at all; the library is the depth, never a requirement. Each card is worded close to its public-domain source, with the source, edition and page under every step, and a link to that page in the library.
- **A few decision trees** where the order matters (bleeding, water, cold).
- **Printable,** one page per card, because a phone dies.
- **The sky strip** on every card: daylight left, north, moonlight.
- **Get the library:** one button lists the ZIMs that fill the guide (zimgit water, knots, medicine, food and post-disaster; WikEM; MedlinePlus; ready.gov; Appropedia; outdoors Stack Exchange; maps of your region) with their sizes, and fetches what fits. A recipe in Create builds a field-manual ZIM from public-domain sources: TC 4-02.1 First Aid (2016), FM 21-76 Survival (1992), the Ranger Handbook (2017).

Content rules: newest source first, the year on everything (a 1957 treatment is never shown as current), "Not medical advice. Call emergency services if you can." on every card, WikEM never in a lay flow, no plant identification, no Hesperian without their written permission, no wikiHow. The first-aid cards get a review by someone medically trained before they ship (decision owed: who).

Size: one card per situation the survival canon ranks (bleeding and breathing first, then warmth and shelter, water, signal and being lost, food, then the disaster checklists), and a tree only where the order of steps matters. That comes to roughly a dozen cards and a handful of trees; the canon sets the number, not us.

Languages: Zimi speaks ten. A first-aid step is not machine-translated: a card shows in a language only once a person has checked it, and until then it shows in English with the language's own library links beneath.

## 4. A best answer that runs on a Pi

Generation on a Pi 4 takes 40-130 s to its first word, so "Ask" waits for the Studio, as Eric wants. What runs in 2-3 s on helix's Pi:

- Search shows a **best answer card** above the results (labelled by its source, never "Answer", because it is a quote): the sentence that answers the question, highlighted inside its passage, "From Water purification, Wikipedia", tapping opens the reader at that spot, with two more passages below.
- Pure Python ranking over passages the search already finds; nothing to install. An optional small reranker (23 MB) sharpens it where it can run.
- It never writes a word of its own, so it cannot invent anything.
- Later, one setting points Zimi at an Ollama you already run (the same way it now uses your qBittorrent), and a short written answer with [1][2] citations appears under the card. Before promising that on a Pi, helix runs one benchmark on his.

## 5. Links

Every view someone might send is a URL that opens that same view: a place on Earth, the sky at a time and place, a Field Guide card, a word, a talk at a moment, a search with its chips. One rule for the hash, applied everywhere. In a disaster on a LAN, "look at this" is a link. Built alongside, view by view, starting with Earth and the sky.

### The solar system, flown

## Raised after the first look (Eric, 2026-10-15, 23:53)

Not for 1.15; candidates for the Zimi + NOMAD release.

- **Night light as its own control, apart from Light/Dark.** Off / Warm / Red. Warm tints whatever theme is on, like Night Shift; Red forces dark and stays total (a white picture costs the dark adaptation Red exists for). Turning on at local sunset, from the Almanac's own sunset for the saved place, is the setting only Zimi can offer. One scope option at most (Warm: pictures too or not); Red has none.
- **Almanac and Field Guide as published ZIMs.** The Field Guide's cards are verbatim quotes with sources, and `build_field_manual` already writes a ZIM, so it can stand alone in any reader. The Almanac's sky, Moon and orrery are JavaScript and run in a ZIM, but tides, satellites, Earth places and the links into your library come from Zimi's server: in Kiwix they would be missing, in Zimi they light up. Cross-ZIM links need a convention (by Q-ID) that Zimi resolves and other readers ignore.
- **Zimi + NOMAD.** Install Project NOMAD on an Ubuntu VM, point Zimi at its storage, and test the real thing (folder reading, live adds and removes, then hardlink adoption) rather than a mocked layout.
